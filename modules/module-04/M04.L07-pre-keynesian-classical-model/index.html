    Lesson M04.L07: The Pre-Keynesian (Classical) Theory of Output

    Module: Macroeconomics in the Short-Run: The Basic Keynesian Model Level: intro Duration: 40 minutes Learning Objective: Explain the pre-Keynesian (classical) theory of output: how the loanable funds market and labour market together determine full-employment output; contrast this with Keynes's critique; apply the rate-of-return investment condition. Provenance: USYD ECON1002 Sem 2 2026 | Bernanke, Olekalns, Frank, Antonovics & Heffetz — Principles of Macroeconomics 5th ed. (BOF5), Ch. 3 §3.5; Ch. 4 §§4.4–4.6; Ch. 5 §5.1; Ch. 14 §§14.2.1–14.2.2

    Why study the pre-Keynesian model?

    Understanding the model Keynes rejected is essential for understanding why Keynesian economics was revolutionary — and why the debate between these two views still shapes macroeconomic policy today. USYD ECON1002 teaches both models explicitly, and exam questions routinely ask you to compare them.

    Explanation

    1. Historical Context

    The pre-Keynesian (or "classical") model dominated economic thinking from the late 19th century. Its central claim is powerful and intuitive: flexible prices and wages ensure markets always clear, so the economy naturally gravitates toward full employment output. There is no persistent involuntary unemployment — just temporary deviations that the market quickly corrects.

    Keynes challenged this in The General Theory (1936), arguing it failed to explain the Great Depression. Today, the pre-Keynesian view is typically treated as the appropriate description of the long run, while the Keynesian model applies to the short run (when prices and wages are sticky).

    2. Investment and the Rate of Return on Capital

    The pre-Keynesian theory begins with firms' investment decisions. A firm considering whether to purchase a new capital good (a machine, building, or piece of equipment) must compare:

    • The rate of return on capital (\(r_k\)): the annual return generated by the new asset as a proportion of its cost
    • The real interest rate (\(r\)): the opportunity cost of funds tied up in the asset
    \[r_k = \frac{R}{p_k}\]

    Where: - \(R\) = net revenue generated by the capital good over the year (net of operating costs) - \(p_k\) = purchase price (market value) of the capital good

    Investment decision rule: Firms invest if \(r_k > r\). They don't invest if \(r_k < r\) (better to put the money in financial assets). The borderline case \(r_k = r\) defines the profit-maximising capital stock.

    Real vs nominal rates: In practice, the relevant comparison uses the real interest rate \(r = i - \pi^e\) (nominal rate minus expected inflation), since both \(R\) and \(p_k\) are expressed in real terms. Real investment requires \(r_k^{real} > r\).

    3. Diminishing Marginal Product of Capital → Downward-Sloping I(r)

    A key assumption is diminishing marginal product of capital (MPK): as a firm accumulates more capital (holding labour and technology fixed), each additional unit of capital produces less extra output. This is the same idea as diminishing returns to any single input.

    Diminishing MPK implies: - As the capital stock \(K\) grows → \(MPK\) falls → \(R\) falls (with constant output price) → \(r_k = R/p_k\) falls - Lower \(r_k\) means the firm's existing capital stops "earning its keep" at lower and lower interest rates

    Result: The desired capital stock \(K^*(r)\) is larger when \(r\) is lower — firms want more capital when borrowing is cheap.

    Since investment \(I\) is the flow that adjusts the actual capital stock toward \(K^*\), the same logic applies:

    \[I = I(r) \quad \text{with } \frac{dI}{dr} < 0\]

    The investment demand curve slopes downward in (\(r\), \(I\)) space.

    Intuition: When real interest rates are high, only high-return projects clear the investment hurdle. When rates are low, more projects become viable — firms invest more.

    4. Saving Supply and the Loanable Funds Market

    The pre-Keynesian model also has a theory of saving. Households face a choice between spending now (consumption) and spending later (saving). A higher real interest rate makes future consumption cheaper relative to present consumption → households save more.

    \[S = S(r, Y) \quad \text{with } \frac{\partial S}{\partial r} > 0\]

    Saving is the supply of loanable funds; investment is the demand for loanable funds. The real interest rate \(r^*\) equilibrates them:

    \[S(r^*, Y) = I(r^*)\]

    Key implications: - If households decide to save more (say, due to greater thriftiness), the supply of loanable funds increases → \(r\) falls → investment rises → the extra saving is matched by extra investment. Output is unchanged. - This is the pre-Keynesian answer to the paradox of thrift — it doesn't happen! Higher saving → lower \(r\) → more investment → same \(Y\).

    The crucial contrast with Keynes

    In the Keynesian model, higher saving → lower consumption demand → lower output → saving falls back to equal investment (the paradox of thrift). Which view is correct depends on whether the interest rate or income is the adjustment mechanism. The pre-Keynesian says interest rate; Keynes says income.

    5. The Traditional Labour Market

    The second pillar of the pre-Keynesian model is a classical labour market analysis.

    Labour demand (\(N^D\)): Firms hire workers until the value of what the last worker produces equals their cost. With diminishing marginal product of labour (MPL):

    \[p \cdot MPL = W \quad \Rightarrow \quad MPL = \frac{W}{p} = (w/p)\]

    As more labour is employed, MPL falls → firms hire less at higher real wages. Labour demand slopes downward in (\(w/p\), \(N\)) space.

    Labour supply (\(N^S\)): Workers choose between work (income) and leisure. A higher real wage makes work relatively more attractive → labour supply slopes upward.

    Equilibrium: With a fully flexible real wage \((w/p)^*\), the market clears at full-employment \(N^*\):

    \[N^D\left(\frac{w}{p}\right)^* = N^S\left(\frac{w}{p}\right)^* = N^*\]

    Full-employment output \(Y_{FE}\) is then determined by the production function:

    \[Y_{FE} = F(K, N^*)\]

    This is the level of output the economy naturally gravitates toward whenever wages are flexible. Any unemployment at this equilibrium is voluntary (workers choosing not to work at the going real wage) or frictional (temporary between jobs) — never involuntary in any persistent sense.

    6. Say's Law and the Two-Part System

    With both markets operating, the pre-Keynesian model reaches a powerful conclusion:

    "Supply creates its own demand" — Say's Law

    At \(Y_{FE}\), income generated in production is sufficient (via consumption and saving/investment) to purchase everything produced. There is no shortfall in aggregate demand. Here's why:

    1. Labour market determines \(N^*\) and thus \(Y_{FE}\) (supply side)
    2. Loanable funds market ensures \(S = I\) at \(r^*\) — saving that leaks from consumption is offset by investment spending (demand side)

    The two markets work together: the labour market sets the output quantity; the loanable funds market ensures aggregate demand matches that quantity. There can be no persistent demand shortfall.

    Involuntary unemployment can only arise if the real wage is stuck above \((w/p)^*\) — for example, because of minimum wages or trade union power preventing downward wage adjustment. The solution is to remove the wage rigidity, not to stimulate demand.

    7. Keynes's Critique

    Keynes's General Theory attacked both pillars:

    Pre-Keynesian claim Keynes's counter
    Real wage adjusts instantly to clear the labour market Nominal wages are sticky downward — workers resist pay cuts; firms fear morale effects. Real adjustment is slow.
    \(r\) adjusts to equate \(S\) and \(I\) Investment is driven by "animal spirits" (business confidence), not the interest rate. When confidence collapses, \(I\) falls and income, not \(r\), adjusts \(S\) down to match.
    Say's Law: supply creates demand Demand is independent of supply in the short run. A collapse in investment or consumer confidence → persistent demand shortfall → output below \(Y_{FE}\).
    Unemployment is voluntary Involuntary unemployment can persist. Workers willing to work at the going wage cannot find jobs — not because wages are "too high" but because there isn't enough demand for output.

    The key difference is the speed of adjustment. Pre-Keynesians say the economy adjusts quickly to \(Y_{FE}\); Keynes said "in the long run we are all dead" — the short-run matters, and markets can fail to self-correct in reasonable time.

    Worked Example

    Given: - Labour supply: \(N^S = 50 + 20 \cdot (w/p)\) - Labour demand: \(N^D = 150 - 20 \cdot (w/p)\) - Production function: \(Y = 5N^{0.5}\) (with capital fixed)

    Find: Equilibrium real wage, employment, and full-employment output.

    Step 1 — Solve the labour market:

    Set \(N^S = N^D\): $\(50 + 20(w/p) = 150 - 20(w/p)\)$ $\(40(w/p) = 100\)$ $\((w/p)^* = 2.5\)$

    Step 2 — Find N*: $\(N^* = 50 + 20 \times 2.5 = 50 + 50 = \mathbf{100}\)$

    Step 3 — Find Y_FE: $\(Y_{FE} = 5 \times (100)^{0.5} = 5 \times 10 = \mathbf{50}\)$

    Interpretation: In the pre-Keynesian model, with a flexible real wage of 2.5, 100 workers are employed and full-employment output of 50 units is produced. Any unemployment at a higher wage (say, minimum wage of 3.5 pushing \(N^D = 80\), \(N^S = 120\)) is a result of that wage floor — not a demand deficiency.

    Investment decision example:

    A firm considers buying a machine costing \(p_k = \$100{,}000\) that generates \(R = \$8{,}000\) net revenue per year (assuming it lasts one year for simplicity).

    \[r_k = \frac{R}{p_k} = \frac{8{,}000}{100{,}000} = 0.08 = 8\%\]
    • If the real interest rate \(r = 5\%\): invest (\(r_k > r\), project earns more than opportunity cost)
    • If the real interest rate \(r = 10\%\): don't invest (\(r_k < r\), better to lend the $100,000 at 10%)
    • If the real interest rate \(r = 8\%\): indifferent (\(r_k = r\), on the margin)

    As the firm expands its capital stock, diminishing MPK means each successive machine generates a lower \(R\) and thus lower \(r_k\) — investment stops expanding when \(r_k = r\) for the last unit.

    Common Misconceptions

    Misconception 1: The pre-Keynesian model says the economy is always at full employment

    Correction: The model says a flexible real wage tends to restore full employment over time. It doesn't claim the economy is always there — only that there are automatic forces pushing it there. The debate is about the speed and reliability of this adjustment.

    Misconception 2: Keynes said saving is bad

    Correction: Keynes identified a paradox — individually rational saving can be collectively self-defeating if it reduces aggregate demand and income. He didn't say saving is intrinsically bad, just that the pre-Keynesian assumption (higher saving → lower r → more investment) may not hold when confidence is low and investment is unresponsive to interest rates.

    Misconception 3: rk and r are the same thing

    Correction: \(r_k = R/p_k\) is the rate of return on a specific capital good — determined by its productivity and price. \(r\) is the real interest rate on financial assets — set in financial markets. The investment decision compares these two rates. They are only equal at the margin (for the last unit of capital invested).

    Practice Prompts

    1. A capital good costs $50,000 and generates $4,500 net revenue per year. The real interest rate is 8%. Should the firm invest? Show your calculation. → Answer: \(r_k = 4500/50000 = 9\% > r = 8\%\). Yes, invest — the project earns more than its opportunity cost.

    2. Explain why the investment demand curve in the pre-Keynesian model slopes downward. What assumption drives this result? → Answer: Diminishing marginal product of capital — as firms accumulate more capital, each additional unit produces less extra output. This means the rate of return \(r_k\) falls as the capital stock grows. So investment is only worthwhile (i.e. \(r_k > r\)) down to a certain capital stock level, which is larger when \(r\) is lower. Hence investment demand is inversely related to the real interest rate.

    3. In the pre-Keynesian model, if households suddenly want to save more (for precautionary reasons), what happens to output? Does a paradox of thrift arise? Contrast with the Keynesian answer. → Answer: Pre-Keynesian: more saving → supply of loanable funds rises → \(r\) falls → investment rises to match saving → \(Y\) unchanged (no paradox of thrift). Keynesian: more saving → lower consumption → lower PAE → lower \(Y\) → \(S\) falls back to equal \(I\) (paradox arises — aggregate saving is unchanged despite individual intent to save more).

    4. What is full-employment output in the pre-Keynesian model? What two conditions must hold for the economy to be there? → Answer: \(Y_{FE}\) is the output level produced when all workers willing to work at the equilibrium real wage are employed. Two conditions: (1) labour market clears — flexible real wage equates \(N^D\) and \(N^S\); (2) loanable funds market clears — \(r\) adjusts so \(S = I\), ensuring aggregate demand = aggregate supply.

    5. A worker is unemployed. Under the pre-Keynesian model, what is the most likely explanation? Under the Keynesian model? → Answer: Pre-Keynesian: the real wage is above the market-clearing level (e.g. minimum wage, union power) → excess supply of labour. Solution: let the real wage fall. Keynesian: insufficient aggregate demand → firms don't need more workers even at current wages → involuntary unemployment not due to a high wage but to low demand. Solution: stimulate aggregate demand.

    Tutorial Questions (USYD ECON1002 Sem 2 2026 — Topic 3)

    These are the actual tutorial questions from Jack's ECON1002 course. Work through them before your tutorial.

    1. Consider a firm deciding on the purchase of a new item of capital equipment which lasts for one year only, which has a purchase price (market value) of \(p_k\) and promises a return for the firm (net of costs) equal to \(R\). Assuming the firm decides to purchase this item of capital equipment, provide a simple expression for the rate of return on the firm's investment.

    *2. How would knowledge of the rate of return and the rate of interest allow the firm to decide whether or not to purchase the new item of capital equipment? Explain.

    *3. (a) If one assumes diminishing marginal productivity of capital and that the price of the output which an item of new capital equipment is used to produce is unchanged, what can one say about the return (\(R\)) from employing additional units of capital as the capital stock gets larger?

    (b) In turn what would your answer in (a) above suggest about the desired size of the capital stock as the rate of interest falls?

    4. What is the implication of your answer to Question 3(a) for the relation between investment and the rate of interest? Explain.

    *5. Suppose we assume that the level of saving is positively related to the real rate of interest.

    (a) In light of the answers to Questions 3 and 4 above, what does this imply about the determination of the real rate of interest?

    (b) In terms of this explanation of the real rate of interest, how might one then allow for the influence of income on saving and in turn on the rate of interest?

    *6. Consider the proposition about diminishing marginal productivity discussed in Question 3 above and suppose this also applies to the use of labour in production. What would this imply about the relation between the real wage rate and the amount of labour producers wish to employ?

    *7. Suppose that the amount of labour people wish to supply is an increasing function of the real wage, what would your answer to Question 6 imply the consequences of a fully flexible real wage in response to differences between demand and supply of labour?

    *8. Consider your answers to Questions 5 and 7 above.

    (a) Consider an initial real wage above the equilibrium level. With a fully flexible real wage, how is any unemployment at the initial real wage affected?

    (b) How is the balance between saving and investment impacted by the flexible real wage and its effect on employment?

    (c) In view of your answers to parts (a) and (b) how is full-employment achieved and what is the role of the rate of interest in this regard?
